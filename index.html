About PolyPainter

Welcome to PolyPainter, a web app for painting on polyhedra! Whatever you paint is repeated all over the solid by some of its symmetries, so a single stroke can become a pattern of 12, 60 or even 120 matching strokes. When you're done, you can export the solid as a net to print, cut out and fold into a paper model, or as a 3D model.

It was inspired by M.C. Escher Kaleidocycles by Doris Schattschneider and Wallace Walker, a book of paper solids covered in Escher's patterns.

The solids

Platonic solids
The five familiar solids whose faces are all the same regular polygon, with the same number of them meeting at every corner: tetrahedron, cube, octahedron, dodecahedron, and icosahedron.
Archimedean solids
Thirteen solids with faces of two or three kinds of regular polygon, arranged the same way around every corner.
Catalan solids
The duals of the Archimedean solids. Each has a face for every corner of its Archimedean partner and a corner for every face. Their faces are all alike, but not regular.
Stellations
Star-shaped solids made by extending the faces of a polyhedron until they meet again. In the menu, you can find the stellated octahedron (also called the stella octangula) and the three stellations of the dodecahedron. The cube and tetrahedron do not have any stellations, but the icosahedron has 59, which are not included here. Stellations can also be created from other polyhedra, not just the Platonic solids.

Symmetry groups

A symmetry of a solid is a way to turn or mirror it so that it ends up looking exactly as it did. Turn a cube a quarter of the way around the line through the centers of two opposite faces, and every face lands where another face was. Mirror it in a plane through its middle, and the same happens. Together, a solid's symmetries form what mathematicians call a group: doing one symmetry after another gives another symmetry, and every symmetry can be undone by one.

PolyPainter paints every dab once for each symmetry in the group you choose, moved by that symmetry. The rings show where all the copies will land. So the number of symmetries in a group, called its order, is the number of copies. It is also the number of matching tiles the group divides the surface into: whatever you paint in one tile appears in every other.

Turning only, or mirroring too

Some groups contain only rotations, and are called chiral. All of their copies are turned versions of what you paint, so they keep its handedness: a spiral that winds clockwise winds clockwise everywhere, and a letter R stays readable. Escher's designs on solids work this way, and so this is the default. The other groups add mirror images, so half of the copies come out reversed, as in a kaleidoscope. To see the difference, paint a small R with each group.

The groups

The cube and the octahedron have the same symmetries, and so do the dodecahedron and the icosahedron, because they are duals. Each group below is all or part of the symmetries of one of the Platonic solids. The letters in parentheses are the Schoenflies notations for the groups.

None
Strokes are not repeated.
Chiral tetrahedral (T) 12 copies · rotations only
The rotations of a tetrahedron: thirds of a turn around each line through a corner and the center of the opposite face, and half turns around each line through the midpoints of two opposite edges.
Full tetrahedral (Td) 24 copies · with mirror images
T with mirror images added: all the symmetries of a tetrahedron. Its six mirror planes each run through one edge and the midpoint of the opposite edge.
Pyritohedral (Th) 24 copies · with mirror images
T with different mirror images added, in the three planes through the center parallel to the faces of a cube. It is named after the mineral pyrite, whose crystals have this symmetry, and it is the largest group that the cube and the dodecahedron have in common.
Chiral octahedral (O) 24 copies · rotations only
The rotations of a cube: quarter turns around the lines through the centers of opposite faces, thirds of a turn around the lines through opposite corners, and half turns around the lines through the midpoints of opposite edges.
Full octahedral (Oh) 48 copies · with mirror images
O with mirror images added: all the symmetries of a cube, with nine mirror planes.
Chiral icosahedral (I) 60 copies · rotations only
The rotations of a dodecahedron: fifths of a turn around the lines through the centers of opposite faces, thirds of a turn around the lines through opposite corners, and half turns around the lines through the midpoints of opposite edges.
Full icosahedral (Ih) 120 copies · with mirror images
I with mirror images added: all the symmetries of a dodecahedron, with 15 mirror planes.

How the groups fit together

Each group is joined by lines to the bigger groups above it that contain it, and the numbers on the left count the copies each row makes. A solid has every group it can reach by going down the lines from its own group, the one its dashed line leads to.

The Symmetry menu lists the groups that the current solid has. The tetrahedron and the solids built from it offer T and Td. Those built from the cube or the octahedron offer T, Td, Th, O and Oh, and those built from the dodecahedron or the icosahedron offer T, Th, I and Ih. A dodecahedron has the rotations of a tetrahedron hidden in it, but no quarter turns and none of the tetrahedron's mirrors. The snub cube, the snub dodecahedron and their Catalan duals come in left- and right-handed forms that are mirror images of each other, so they offer rotations only.

What the names mean

The letters, called Schoenflies notation, name the solid that a group belongs to: T for tetrahedron, O for octahedron and I for icosahedron, with h or d added for groups with mirror images. The digits under the menu are John Conway's orbifold notation, which describes the pattern itself. Each digit is a kind of point the pattern turns around, and says how many times the pattern repeats in one full turn: in 532 there are points it repeats around five times, three times and twice. An asterisk stands for mirrors. A digit after it marks a point where that many mirrors cross, and a digit before it a center of rotation that lies on no mirror. So in *532, mirrors cross in fives, threes and twos, and in 3*2 there are threefold centers away from the mirrors and points where two mirrors cross.
